One idea for how family history stories get told on the website is through horizontal scroll. The broader site concept is leaning toward pages that live within a single fixed aspect ratio frame where you don't scroll down. When you click into a specific family story, it would open into a horizontal scrolling experience where you move sideways through the narrative.

The reason horizontal scroll feels right for family stories is that the movement maps to chronology. You're moving forward through a sequence of events, and the horizontal axis reinforces that like a timeline. These stories have clear arcs with distinct beats that can be paced across horizontal space. The format lets each moment in a story get its own room without stacking things vertically like a blog post or an archive page.

A big part of why this format is worth exploring is that it changes how historical content registers. The family stories are real and interesting on their own, but the risk with presenting old family history on a website is it defaults to feeling like a heritage exhibit. A fluid horizontal scroll with an editorial layout makes the experience feel contemporary and designed, even when the subject matter is 150 years old.

The reference in the bank (Horizontal Editorial Narrative Layout) is the closest example of how this could work. Large serif titles and numbered sections anchor the narrative. Body text is offset and positioned asymmetrically rather than stacked in blocks. Images vary in size and placement across the horizontal plane, some overlapping, some isolated, handled more like editorial assets in a magazine spread than a grid. The commentary on that reference specifically calls out the graphic system, the way titles and subtitles and numbers work together, how the paragraph text is offset, and how the images are laid out throughout.

What lives inside the scroll, whether it's photography, illustration, generated imagery, typography, or some combination, follows from whatever visual directions get worked out for the brand. This concept is about horizontal scroll as the structural format for how family stories get experienced on the site.

**Testing note:** The format alone does not make historical content feel contemporary. Early tests put old-feeling imagery and illustration styles (charcoal sketches, heritage typography, archival treatments) inside a horizontal scroll and the result still looked like a museum exhibit. The scroll format is the structure. The visual content inside it has to be contemporary too. Whatever imagery, graphics, and type treatments go into these stories need to follow the same rules as the rest of the brand — no archival, no scrapbook, no heritage-exhibit feeling — regardless of how old the subject matter is.

How someone enters the horizontal scroll depends on the larger site architecture. It could open from a node on a map, a link from another page, a click within a canvas. The entry point is a separate question from the format itself.
