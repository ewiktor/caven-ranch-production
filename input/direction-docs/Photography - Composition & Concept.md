## Photography: Composition and Concept

Several composition concepts have emerged from the references and direction conversations. They address different subject contexts and different ideas about how the camera relates to what it's shooting.

### People

People are not posed, not looking at the camera, not shot like stock photography. Faces are turned away, cropped out, too far to read, or taken out of focus through motion. The camera observes from a distance. The feeling is voyeuristic: the viewer is brought into a moment from a detached perspective that communicates "when you are here, you are undisturbed." The moment is captured but we don't get too much into the scene.

This works across a range of proximity. At the close end, faceless crops of hands, laps, midsections engaged in activity. Intimate but anonymous. At a middle distance, people are embedded in the space through frame-within-a-frame compositions, shot through doorways or thresholds, where the space carries as much weight as the person and the person is not the total central focal point. At the far end, wide or high-angle perspectives where people are small in the frame, part of the environment rather than the subject.

The consistent idea is that the viewer can imagine themselves in the moment rather than observing someone else's.

### Spaces Without People

Interiors and exteriors shot as if someone just left or is about to sit down. A bench with scattered pillows. A desk with a glass and a notebook. An unmade bed seen through a doorway. A reading nook with a book set down. The space has just been occupied or is ready to be occupied. The viewer can imagine themselves slotting in.

One mode is tight, angled, slightly off-kilter. Focused in on one specific spot, a chair, a corner, a nook. Close and tactile. The other mode is wider and more evenly composed, showing a full patio or lounge setup with layered depth: foreground texture, mid-ground focal space, background horizon or architecture.

The spaces are not perfect. Pillows aren't fluffed. Things are slightly askew. It's lived-in rather than staged.

### Architecture and Property in Landscape

The buildings are captured within the natural environment, not isolated from it. The architecture doesn't dominate. It enters the frame from the side or sits low while nature takes up most of the composition.

### Same Scene Across the Day

A content concept about shooting the same scene or similar scenes across major phases of the day: sunrise, peak sun, late afternoon, twilight, night. The same setting from different angles or the same angle at different times, communicating the passage of time and the idea of paying attention to the day as it passes. This ties into the overall experience concept of going out here to slow down and be present.

### Shooting Through Things

A recurring compositional device across multiple concepts. Shooting through doorways, thresholds, windows, foliage, architectural elements. Using layers in the composition, foreground, mid-ground, background, to create depth, bring the viewer into the scene, and add intrigue. This shows up both in how people are shot (through thresholds from a distance) and how spaces are shot (foreground grasses framing a patio beyond, architectural frames around interior nooks).

### Interior-Specific Composition

The references for interior shots are specifically not about showcasing furniture, finishes, or specific design details. They're about how to frame and angle the shot to communicate the experience of being inside the space.

A major idea is the inside-out relationship. The cabins have large glass windows and open thresholds that connect interior to exterior. The composition uses those openings to split the viewer's attention between the sheltered interior and the landscape beyond. You shoot from inside looking out, or you use the threshold itself as a vertical anchor that divides the frame, showing both the living space and the deck or landscape it opens onto simultaneously. The point is that the interior and exterior are not separate experiences in these cabins. The framing should show them as continuous.

Another idea is architectural layering and depth. Shooting through the space rather than at it. Positioning the camera so that a foreground wall, a curved cutout, a corridor, or a doorway frames what's beyond it, creating a sense of discovery as your eye moves through the layers. A human figure might appear in the background for scale and narrative, but the composition is about the spatial journey from where you're standing to what's deeper in the space or beyond it.

Light behavior is a big focus. How diagonal sunlight pours through the large windows and creates sharp geometric shadows on vertical timber cladding. How the angle of the shot is chosen to capture where the light hits the wood or the floor, making the light itself the focal point rather than any specific object. The camera follows what the light is doing to the architecture at a given time of day.

The framing uses the strong vertical and horizontal lines of the architecture, the timber paneling, the ceiling angles, the window frames, as a compositional grid. The shots feel structured and intentional, with the geometry of the cabin organizing the image. But within that rigid structure, the mood is warm and quiet because of how the light and material textures fill the space.
