Marsha is the client's mother. She's a conservationist who tracks animals, plants, and ecological details on the property. There's a full list of animals that came from the client already in the knowledge base.

The idea behind Marsha's List is using her name and her perspective as a way for the brand to communicate about nature and conservation on the property. Rather than the brand speaking in a generic editorial voice about ecology, Marsha becomes a possible lens. Her observations, her tracking of species, her relationship to the land as a steward.

The subject matter this would cover: the animals on the property, native plants and grasses, the conservation deeds the family put in place (where large portions of the ranch can never be built on, even by future owners), the work they did with a state conservation association to bring back endangered bird species by removing invasive trees and replanting native grasses. All of this is real and documented.

One idea for how this takes shape is a dedicated page on the website built as a free-flowing canvas rather than a traditional vertically-stacked layout. A large open space you move around in. Photographs of the land, the ecology, the animals — scattered at varied scales across a clean background. You click into things and they open up.

The canvas is a contemporary digital layout. The content is photographs. Not pressed flowers, not scanned specimens, not illustrations of plants, not family artifacts, not torn paper, not handwritten notes. Every time the concept was described using those kinds of objects, the output came back looking like a scrapbook or a museum exhibit. The "naturalist's desk" metaphor is retired. What photography treatment these images get is a separate decision that depends on where the broader photography direction lands.

The canvas is not the only way Marsha's List could work. The core value of the idea is the framing device: a real person's name attached to real ecological knowledge, delivered in a personal and observational tone rather than an institutional or educational one. How that actually gets built out remains open.

There was discussion about whether family history stories (the grandfather falling off the horse, the World War I helmet, the death story) would live inside Marsha's List or get separated. The thinking leans toward separating them. Marsha's List would stay focused on nature, conservation, and the living property. Family stories could get their own format, possibly horizontal scroll sequences.

But we could wrap them together.

Wherever this lands, it functions as a relationship-building piece. It's not meant to sell or convert. It's where someone already interested goes deeper into the property and the family's values without being marketed to.
