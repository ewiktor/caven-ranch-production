## Illustration Style

Illustration is a primary graphic element across the brand. It shows up on the website where the direction is to have a large illustration above the fold on each page (following the Ett Hem model), within the horizontal scroll family stories where individual story beats could be illustrated, potentially within the Marsha's List canvas, and in the coyote brand character which has its own illustration directions in a separate doc.

The style has not been decided. What exists is one hard constraint, some early tonal thinking, and a large bank of references exploring different approaches to technique, texture, line work, and shading.

### What's Off the Table

Standard pen and ink illustration. Every boutique hotel does it. Whatever the illustration style ends up being, it needs to not feel like the default for this category.

Also off the table: charcoal sketches, engravings, and woodblock prints. These were tested and every output came back looking archival, heritage, old — like something from a history book or a nature journal. The constraint isn't just "not pen-and-ink." It's that the illustration must read as contemporary. If it looks like it belongs in a museum or a scrapbook, it's wrong.

### What the References Show

The illustration references in the bank cover a wide range of techniques. All were saved with commentary focused on style, approach, line work, texture, shading, and detail level. The range includes:

- Gestural ink line work with varying stroke weights, loose and expressive, minimal rendering, form defined through movement rather than precision.
- Crayon or oil pastel texture with visible wax grain, scribbled directional shading, raw and tactile.
- Charcoal and graphite with fine cross-hatching and stippling, matte finish, more controlled and detailed.
- Thick impasto brushstrokes, painterly, heavy texture with visible paint ridges, form defined by the weight of the strokes rather than line.
- Ink wash and watercolor where pigment bleeds and pools create shading naturally, minimal line work, silhouette-driven.
- Lithographic and woodblock-inspired approaches with stippled, porous texture, bold black ink, edges that feel printed rather than drawn.
- Cobalt blue on cream ceramic, fine-point detailed cross-hatching, a Delftware-inspired approach.
- High-contrast silhouettes with distressed, jagged edges, primitive and heavy, minimal internal detail.
- White impasto or ink on dark backgrounds (indigo, navy), where light strokes define form against a void, with dry-brush effects and visible bristle marks.

None of these have been chosen. They represent the territory being explored. The common thread across most of them is that they feel made by hand, have visible texture from the medium (whether ink, paint, crayon, or print process), and lean toward expressive mark-making over clean rendering.

### Tone and Use

The client described a general vision for how the brand communicates its stories as "deadpan slapstick comedy, but elevated." The reference for tone was New Yorker cartoons, not the drawing style specifically, but how those cartoons handle dramatic subjects with brevity and dry humor. That thinking came up in the context of needing a way to tell family stories (a guy falling off a horse in Mexico, a WWI helmet hanging in a garden with flowers growing out of it) without it feeling serious, dramatic, or old, and without using photography. Illustration was the answer for those moments because it gives a different register.
