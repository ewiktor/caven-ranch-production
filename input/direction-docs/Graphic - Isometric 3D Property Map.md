## Isometric 3D Property Map

The idea is a map of the property rendered in isometric 3D, where the dimensionality itself is contemporary and digital, but the texture and surface of the map feels old and characterful. Grainy, hand-drawn, hatched, organic. Not glossy CGI. The contrast between those two things, old surface and new dimension, is what gives it edge and makes it feel more human.

The property experience is physically spread across the land. The cabins are clustered in one area. You go somewhere else to swim in the river. A safari tent is being built in another spot for lunch. You move between places across the property over the course of a stay. The map ties each experience to where it actually happens on the land, showing the individual moments you have and where they take place.

One idea is making it interactive on the website with clickable nodes at specific locations that open into imagery or information about what you do at that spot. This would make it a web experience you navigate rather than just a graphic you look at.

### What the References Cover

There are references in the bank for both the surface treatment and the isometric format.

For surface treatment, the collected references range from fine-line topographic illustration with hatching and contour lines representing terrain, to vintage cartographic styles with cross-hatching and stippling, to very minimal approaches like a single monoline path on a heavily textured canvas background. Some treat the map more like an editorial object, isolated in negative space with fine callout lines and serif labels. The common thread across all of them is that the surface feels made by hand rather than generated by software, with visible texture, grain, or mark-making.

For the isometric format, references show both a floating landmass approach with numbered waypoints and clean data overlays, and a more layered approach where textured organic terrain sits underneath a modern UI layer of floating cards and interface elements.

The surface treatment and the 3D format haven't been pinned down yet. The references give a range of territory for both.

### Testing Notes

Initial AI testing of the isometric format produced crude, thick, bulbous results. Trees were fat and oversized. Structures were chunky. Everything lacked refinement, subtlety, and realistic proportions. The rendering quality was the problem — it looked like amateur 3D work, not a skilled architectural visualization.

The references that work have fine, delicate, precise rendering. Trees are thin and proportionally realistic. Tonal gradation is subtle. Every element is rendered with care and specificity. The difference is craftsmanship — the same difference between a refined architectural masterplan and a first-year student's homework.

The prompt never described the quality of rendering. It described what to show but not how finely to render it. Without language about fineness, delicacy, and realistic proportional detail, AI defaults to thick, crude, oversized forms.

**Promptable language that should be present in any isometric map prompt:**

- fine, delicate, precisely rendered terrain with realistic proportions
- trees rendered thin and detailed at correct scale to the land
- subtle tonal gradation across the terrain surface
- high-quality architectural visualization level of refinement
- elements rendered with specificity and restraint, nothing oversized or crude

**Perspective and implementation:** The final version of the map may involve multiple perspectives of the same terrain — a straight-on aerial top-down of the full property, a side-angle isometric mid-range, a close-up top-down detail of a specific area like the cabin cluster or river bend. These would be separate frames generated individually, not one image. The eventual implementation could use AI video tools or animation to transition between these frames as a single continuous experience on the site. When prompting, each perspective should be its own prompt with its own angle and crop. Do not try to combine multiple zoom levels into a single image — that will produce garbage.

Two surface treatment routes are still alive and untested:

1. **Photorealistic:** Satellite-quality or high-end architectural visualization. Real terrain textures, real topography. The landmass as a refined, continuous surface isolated in clean space.
2. **Hand-drawn/hatched texture:** The original direction — grainy, organic surface with visible mark-making on a contemporary 3D form. This has not been tested yet.

Both need to be tried before either is ruled out.
