# Family History

**Source type:** kb_entry

- The Caven family effort to create a destination on this ranch is being led by Andrew Caven, great-great-great grandson of the original settler, John H. Robinson.
- **John Hermy (Henry) Robinson** is the patriarch.
  - He was born **August 25, 1815, in London, England**. Baptized at Westminster St. Margaret in Middlesex. His parents were Michael Robinson and Mary. He immigrated to America **before 1836** as a young man, barely 20. His listed occupations before Austin include **mariner** and running a **bakery business in New Orleans**. He married **Elizabeth Sheridan** on **July 10, 1836, in New Orleans**. She lived from 1817 to 1902.
  - Then he shows up in **Little Rock, Arkansas in 1840** as a merchant. He's there for over a decade. On **February 9, 1853**, a fire in downtown Little Rock **destroyed his store and merchandise**. That fire is almost certainly what pushed him to Texas — because by **1855** he's residing in **Austin, Travis County, Texas**, and by **1856** he's running a **drygoods and grocery business in Austin**.
  - John was a **City Alderman of Austin**. Not once. Repeatedly, across nearly two decades:
    - **1860–62**: Alderman, City of Austin
    - **1867**: Alderman
    - **1871–72**: Alderman
    - **1873**: Alderman, Ward 7
    - **1874–77**: Alderman, Ward 9
  - From **1887 to 1896** — basically the last decade of his life — he was **President of the Washington Steam Fire Engine Company No. 1**. That's Austin's fire company.
  - His store was on **Congress Avenue between Pecan and Pine Street** — that's the main axis of downtown Austin. The business was called **"John H. Robinson and Son"** — the Son being Alonzo. This operated from at least **1872 to 1878** per the records.
  - He **retired as a grocer around 1889–90**. His probate was filed **February 20, 1896**. He died **March 5, 1897, of apoplexy** (stroke). He was buried **at Oakwood Cemetery** where the city's founding-era families are buried, including multiple governors and state officials.
  - John and Elizabeth had ten children:
    - William Thomas Robinson (1837–1920)
    - John Henry Robinson (1839–1934)
    - Mary Amelia Robinson (1842–1872)
    - Alfred Henry Robinson (1844–1885)
    - Elizabeth "Lizzie" Robinson (1847–1920)
    - Lillian H. "Lillie" Robinson (1849–1867)
    - Alonzo Robinson (1852–1936)
    - Austin Mortimer Robinson (1856–1912)
    - Laura Ada Robinson (1859–1945)
    - Eugene Bremond Robinson (1862–1949)
- The Robinson family was directly connected to the **Bremond family**. The Bremonds were one of the wealthiest and most powerful families in Austin. **John Bremond Sr.** settled in Austin in 1846 and built a fortune in merchandising and banking. His sons **Eugene Bremond** (1832–1910) and **John Bremond Jr.** were the main players. The family built a cluster of Victorian mansions on a single downtown block — West 7th, West 8th, Guadalupe, and San Antonio Streets — known as the **Bremond Block**. It's a National Register Historic District today.
  - The connection came through two marriages going in opposite directions. John and Elizabeth's daughter **Mary Amelia Robinson** (1842–1872) married **Eugene Bremond**
  - Then John and Elizabeth's son **Alfred Henry Robinson** (1844–1885) married **Pauline Bremond** (1849–1935) — Eugene's sister — on March 30, 1869.
  - When Alfred died in 1885, Eugene Bremond had a house built for his widowed sister Pauline Robinson at 705 San Antonio Street—on the Bremond Block itself. That house is now known as the **Catherine Robinson House**, passed to Pauline's daughter.
  - The Robinson family's own home was at the northwest corner of Rio Grande and 7th Street — one block from the Bremond Block.
- **Alonzo Robinson** (1852–1936) took over the family grocery business in 1884. He married **Laura H. Watson** (1853–1915). He bought the ranch land in the Hill Country.
  - According to the family, Alonzo was a surveyor for the state of Texas. He identified the Hill Country property as his favorite piece of land during his surveying work and was able to buy it for very little.
- **Ralph Watson Robinson** (1886–1978), Alonzo and Laura Watson's son. Married **Esther Woodward** (1890–1970). Lived on the ranch his entire adult life, back when the property spanned roughly 5,000 acres. The property extended from what is now the state park to the current boundaries. He is the central character of the property's history.
  - Around 1902, when Ralph was about 16, he went to Mexico to work on a 100,000-acre ranch. Out riding one day, a snake spooked his horse, which bucked him off and ran away with his gun. He was stranded in the desert with nothing. He wandered for two full days before finding his gun miles from where he had been thrown, an event the family describes as "divine intervention." He hunted and lived off the land for several more days before making it back to the ranch headquarters.
  - Ralph served in World War I. He was part of the last cavalry unit of the army. He spent time in France as the conflict wound down. Before he left he had a French artist engrave a spent artillery shell with a scene of cavalry riders. He also brought back his combat helmet and hung it in the garden at the ranch, which he tended carefully. The shell and Ralph’s combat helmet are still at the ranch today.
  - Ralph and Esther had three children: **Pauline "Polly" Robinson** (1921–2011), **James Anthony Robinson** (1922–deceased), and **Laura Robinson** (1926–2018).
  - In 1955, Ralph and Esther were living in a house on the Pedernales River on the property. A historic flood hit in the middle of the night. They got into their car. The floodwater swept the car away and separated Ralph, Esther, and the dog in the darkness. Ralph clung to a tree overnight. The next morning, he found that Esther and the dog had both survived in a different tree. The foundation of that house is still visible on the property, at a shocking distance from the river, demonstrating the power and severity of flash floods in the region.
  - A convict escaped from the Johnson City jail. The last sighting was near the ranch. Ralph got spooked and started cleaning and loading his guns. A shotgun discharged and shot him. It did not kill him, but he was taken to a hospital in Austin. He survived the wound. The doctor told him he could not return to living alone at the ranch. Within a day or two of that news, he died. He was 92.
  - Ralph was an eccentric and at times difficult person. The ranch was everything to him. The family's read on his death: it was not the gunshot that killed him, it was the thought that he would not go back.
  - When Ralph died in 1978, there was no will. He did not believe in lawyers. The property had to be broken up.
- **Laura Robinson** (1926–2018), was Ralph and Esther's youngest child. She graduated from Stephen F. Austin High School in Austin, then from the University of Texas, where she was a member of Pi Beta Phi sorority. She was working at the Texas State Capitol when she met **Walter Thomas Caven** on a blind date. Neither of them was actually there for the other. Walter had come with a different woman and Laura had come with a different man. They married in 1951.
  - Laura was a member of the Junior League of Austin and served as a delegate to a regional convention of the national association in 1954. She is described in the family record as a strong community volunteer whose main focus was always her family.
  - **Walter Thomas Caven** (1920–2007). Born 23 November 1920 in Marshall, Texas, Harrison County. Son of William and Ella Sedberry Caven. His father was an attorney who represented the railroads. Marshall was the shop town for the Texas and Pacific Railroad.
  - Walter graduated from Marshall High School in 1937. He was president of his senior class and of the National Honor Society. He attended the University of Texas beginning in 1941, where he studied in Plan II, UT's elite interdisciplinary honors program, and then entered law school. At UT he was a member of Alpha Tau Omega fraternity, the Cowboys honorary service group, and was selected as a Goodfellow.
  - Following Pearl Harbor, he left school and enlisted in the Army. He served with the 7th Division infantry as a platoon leader. He was wounded in action on 15 November 1944 during the Battle of Leyte Gulf in the Philippines. He received the **Silver Star**, **Purple Heart**, and **Bronze Star**.
  - He returned to Austin and graduated from the University of Texas Law School in 1947. The State Supreme Court issued his license to practice law that same year.
  - He ran for the **Texas House of Representatives** from Harrison County in 1948 as a Democrat. He won and served one term in the Texas Legislature in 1949. The original campaign card still exists. It reads "Dependable, Capable, Qualified" around his photograph, with the Allied Printing Trades Union Council mark from Marshall, Texas.
  - After his term he went to work for the Railroad Association. His father had practiced railroad law and Walter had grown up in a railroad town, so this was a natural fit. He served as general counsel and lobbyist for the **Texas Railroad Association** for 33 years. After retiring from the railroads he became of counsel at **Akin, Gump, Strauss, Houer & Feld** for eight years.
  - He served as president of the **Headliners Club** in 1968. The Headliners Club is Austin's premier private club for media, politics, and business. He was active in Democratic politics and was a supporter of Price Daniel Jr. in his pursuit of the position of Speaker of the Texas House in 1972.
  - From the family record: "He lived life large, loved golf, hunting, Texas football, his place in the country and his many friends and family."
  - Laura and Walter had two children: **Walter Thomas Jr. "Tom" Caven**, born 10 December 1952, and **Caroline Caven**, born 1 June 1955 in Austin.
- **Walter Thomas Jr. "Tom" Caven**, or Tom, is Andrew's father. He plays in a bluegrass band. Andrew's mother is **Marsha Caven**. She is a master naturalist who completed a formal training program in the field. She keeps a running list of the wildflowers and plants on the property. She has deep knowledge of the flora and fauna of the Hill Country.
  - The family has placed roughly 100 acres along the river into a conservation easement with the Nature Conservancy. They have conducted wildlife surveys for endangered species on the property. The golden-cheeked warbler, a federally endangered bird, is present on the ranch and protected by the easement. The family also removed cattle from the land to let it rest and plans to bring them back under a regenerative grazing program.
- The Robinson Grocery building is still standing on Congress Avenue in Austin. The mosaic tile floor inside the entrance still reads "Robinson and Scott." The building is still operating as a commercial space.
- Other branches of the Robinson family remained prominent in Austin. One branch owned a ranch just north of town that was sold when Apple built its second headquarters there.
