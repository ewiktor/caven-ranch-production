# Design standards

When you design anything, you have to pay extra attention to:

- **Typography** — selection, kerning, tracking, size, weight, line-height, pairings, casing, hierarchy between levels
- **Spacing** — spacing scale, grouping/proximity, relative ratios between gaps, padding, margins, alignment to a rhythm, placement of empty space
- **Colors** — selection, pairings, number of colors, accent placement, derived neutrals/tints, contrast, temperature consistency
- **Composition & layout** — focal point, eye path, visual weight/balance, scale contrast, grid, alignment, grouping, negative space as an element, tension/asymmetry
- **Graphic elements & detailing** — lines, borders, dividers, icons, labels/chrome: what earns its place vs decoration, stroke weights, corner radii
- **Hierarchy & emphasis** — what reads first/second/third, how strongly, and through which lever: size vs weight vs color vs position
- **Content-to-form fit** — does the layout choice actually express what the content means, or is it a template with words poured in
- **Craft finishing** — optical alignment, baseline alignment, consistent detail sizing: the last 5% polish pass

## A guide, not rules

Everything below is a guide, not a rule. These are observations about why good compositions hold together, drawn from references the user has pointed at. They are here to sharpen judgment, not to be checked off. A design can break any one of them on purpose and be better for it. If one of these fights the specific thing you are making, the thing you are making wins. Do not treat them as locks, and do not cite them as reasons.

**One idea first, then let every lever serve it.** The strongest reference designs decide what the thing is about before touching type, color, or spacing, and then every choice carries that one idea. In the art-auction reference, the idea was "framed object under glass," and the warm mat, the connoisseur serif, the museum-label caps, and the corner brackets all said that. When the levers have no shared idea to serve, the result reads as generic minimalism with decoration added. Before making anything, it is worth being able to say in a sentence what the piece is about, and then asking of each element whether it carries that.

**Separation can come from space or a change of value, not only from a line.** A common reference move is to divide sections by changing the background panel (a darker tint, then a lighter tint, then white) rather than drawing a rule between them. The panel change is the divider. Reach for a hairline only when space and value genuinely cannot do the job. A line that is not separating anything is decoration.

**Color is often one hue at a few values, with the photograph carrying the saturation.** Several references the user likes run on a single temperature: one hue used as two or three tints, plus a near-black for text and a warm gray for secondary text, and then the image supplies the only saturated color in the frame. The layout stays quiet so the photograph lands. This is one reason a flat ground plus a single bright accent rule can feel thin: there is a color scheme but nothing derived, no tint doing work.

**Type reads best when each family has one job and holds it.** A serif for the voice, a sans for support, a mono or small caps for chrome and labels, each kept to its role across the whole piece. The families do not swap jobs from section to section. This is different from choosing new fonts per board; the point is a fixed set of roles that the content moves through.

**Hierarchy is stronger when levels use different levers, not just size.** Size, weight, color, casing, and position are all available. A byline that keeps the name in black and drops the date to gray at the same size is splitting by color. Small tracked caps set a label apart by casing and position. Leaning on size alone for every level flattens the result.

**Containment means something.** A matted, inset image reads as a curated object. A full-bleed image reads as immersion. Whether an image is framed, bled, or floated is a decision about what it means, not a default. The reference mats its image because the subject is art under glass; another piece bleeds its image because the subject is being inside a place.

**Emptiness is placed, not left over.** In the calm reference layouts the empty area is deliberate and often the largest single element. It sits where it does on purpose, to hold a focal point or to let one thing breathe. Empty space that is simply what remains after the content is arranged reads differently from empty space that was positioned.

**One hard lane holds a text group together.** Eyebrow, headline, deck, and byline hanging off a single left edge, with trailing elements sitting on the right edge of that same lane, is what makes a stack read as one considered unit rather than several loose pieces.
